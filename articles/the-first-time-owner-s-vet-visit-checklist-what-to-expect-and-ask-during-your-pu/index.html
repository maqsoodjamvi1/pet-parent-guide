Bringing a new puppy home is an incredible milestone, but if you are like I was during my first pup’s initial checkup, you are probably a little nervous about the upcoming veterinary visit. Walking into a busy clinic with a wiggly, unvaccinated eight-week-old ball of fur can feel overwhelming.

When I took my golden retriever mix, Barnaby, to his first appointment, I realized that preparing ahead of time makes all the difference. Vets are busy, and clinic rooms can be loud, sensory-heavy environments. Having a clear plan helps you advocate for your puppy and get the exact information you need. Here is a practical roadmap of what to expect and what to ask so you can walk into that exam room with confidence.

What to Bring in Your Puppy Bag

Preparation starts before you even leave your house. Clinics see a lot of animals, and keeping your puppy comfortable and secure is your first job.

Pack a dedicated puppy bag with these essentials:

  • Medical records: Bring any paperwork from the breeder, shelter, or rescue, including initial deworming dates and vaccine history.
  • A stool sample: Vets almost always test for parasites. Try to collect a fresh sample (less than 12 hours old) in a sealed plastic baggie.
  • High-value treats: Bring tiny, smelly treats like soft training bites or pieces of boiled chicken to keep your puppy distracted and create a positive association with the exam table.
  • Paper towels and wipes: Accidents happen in the waiting room or exam table, and cleaning up quickly reduces your stress.
  • A secure carrier or snug leash: Keep small puppies off the waiting room floor, as their immune systems are still developing. Use a carrier or hold them in your lap.

What Happens During the Physical Exam

When the veterinary technician brings you into the room, they will usually start by taking your puppy’s weight and asking about their general demeanor at home. When the veterinarian enters, they will perform a nose-to-tail physical assessment.

Knowing what they are checking can help you observe your puppy more closely at home. The vet will listen closely to your puppy’s heart and lungs to check for congenital murmurs or respiratory issues. They will look inside the mouth to check the bite alignment and baby teeth, feel the abdomen for organ size and hernias (which are common in puppies), and inspect the skin and coat for fleas, ticks, or dry patches.

This is also when they will look into the ears for mites and check the eyes. Expect the vet to handle your puppy’s paws, tail, and mouth firmly. This isn’t just for medical reasons; it gets the puppy used to being handled, which makes future grooming and nail trims much easier.

Critical Questions to Ask Your Veterinarian

Do not rely on memory while you are in the room. Write your questions down in your phone notes ahead of time. Beyond discussing the initial round of vaccines, here are four specific questions I always recommend asking:

  1. “What specific parasite preventative do you recommend for our zip code?” Parasite risks vary wildly by region. Fleas, ticks, heartworms, and intestinal worms require specific, weight-based preventatives that are safe for growing bodies.
  2. “Based on my puppy’s breed mix, what structural or joint issues should I monitor as they grow?” Large breeds have different nutritional needs and joint vulnerabilities than small breeds. Ask about safe exercise limits to protect developing growth plates.
  3. “Can you show me the safest way to brush my puppy’s teeth?” Starting dental care early saves thousands of dollars in veterinary dental cleanings later in life. Ask for a demonstration on proper technique and toothpaste safety (never use human toothpaste, as it contains fluoride and xylitol).
  4. “What specific human foods are toxic that I need to keep out of reach?” Aside from the obvious chocolate and grapes, ask about pantry staples like garlic, onions, macadamia nuts, and artificial sweeteners like xylitol.

Understanding the Vaccine Schedule and Costs

Puppies need a series of boosters because their mother’s antibodies gradually wear off, leaving their immune systems vulnerable. Usually, puppies receive DHPP (Distemper, Hepatitis, Parainfluenza, and Parvovirus) boosters every three to four weeks from about six to sixteen weeks of age, along with the rabies vaccine around twelve to sixteen weeks.

Ask the vet for an estimate of the total cost for the complete puppy series so you can budget accordingly. Be prepared for your puppy to be a little sleepy or sore for 24 hours after receiving their shots—this is a normal immune response, much like a human toddler getting a vaccine.

As you plan your next steps in your puppy-raising journey, establishing a good relationship with your veterinary team is the single best foundation you can build.
